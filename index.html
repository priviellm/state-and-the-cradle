The same Mexican president said both. This page looks at how governments decide how many children their citizens should have, and at the laws, money, medals, clinics and buildings they use to push the number up or down. The two main cases are Mexico and the Soviet Union.

Why this topic

Origin: ITAM, “Problemas sociales”

Before transferring to UH, I studied at ITAM in Mexico City. In a social problems course there, I read a book about the methods governments use to manage their populations: how public services and incentives are arranged so that people have more children, or fewer. The book compared several states, including Mexico and the Soviet Union.

Assignment briefChoose a topic you consider knowledge worth having, research it, and present it.

What stayed with me is that a decision most people think of as the most private one in their lives is, in aggregate, something states plan for and try to steer. They rarely do it by force alone. More often they change the conditions around the decision: what contraception costs, what a family gets paid, who gets an apartment first, whether there is a nursery at the factory.

As an architecture student, that last category matters to me. Housing, clinics and childcare are built things. When a government sets the size of an apartment or decides who qualifies for it, it is also making an assumption about family size, and sometimes trying to change it.

The toolkit

Five levers, used in both directions

Demographers group these interventions under population policy. Policies meant to raise births are called pronatalist; policies meant to lower them are antinatalist or, more politely, family-planning policies. The instruments are largely the same. Only the direction changes.

Mexico: from populate to “let’s be fewer”

DOF, 7 enero 1974

The pronatalist decades, 1936–1970

After the Revolution, Mexican leaders saw an empty country. The 1936 Ley General de Población set out to grow the population: more marriages and births, lower mortality, the return of Mexicans living abroad, and selective immigration. The sale of contraceptives was prohibited, and some states taxed unmarried adults. The 1947 law kept the same direction. President Miguel Alemán argued that half a million new Mexicans a year secured the country’s economic development.

1936 lawInterior Secretary Ignacio García Téllez: growth should come from “el crecimiento natural” of the Mexican people.

It worked in the sense that the population boomed, though falling mortality did more than any law. By 1974 Mexico had about 60 million people, growing 3.5% a year, fast enough to double in 20 years. Women averaged about six children.

The reversal, 1970–1976

Luis Echeverría campaigned in 1969 on “gobernar es poblar.” Within a year of taking office he was warning that population growth could outrun economic development. In February 1973 the government lifted the ban on selling and advertising contraceptives. On 7 January 1974 a new Ley General de Población took effect with the opposite goal: regulate the size, structure and distribution of the population. It created the Consejo Nacional de Población (CONAPO) to plan it. The same year, Article 4 of the Constitution was amended to give every person the right to decide freely, responsibly and in an informed way on the number and spacing of their children.

The law came with a media machine. The first campaign in 1974 printed three million leaflets and half a million posters and ran on radio and television. Its slogan, “Vámonos haciendo menos,” played on “let’s become fewer” and “let’s become less”: less passive, less macho, less irresponsible, less ostentatious. Later campaigns followed: “La familia pequeña vive mejor” (1975), “Señora, usted decide si se embaraza” (1976), and “Tener hijos es cuestión de pareja” (1978). Family-planning readings entered the free sixth-grade textbooks. CONAPO’s logo showed a family of four, a picture of the target of two children.

Mexican scienceNorethindrone, the progestin in early birth-control pills, was first synthesized in Mexico City in 1951, from compounds derived from a wild Mexican yam.

By 2014 fertility was 2.2 and growth was about 1% a year. Mexico had about 127 million people in 2014, against 1970s projections of more than 150 million. Demographers still debate how much of the drop the policy caused and how much urbanization, women’s schooling and work would have produced anyway.

The method mix raises its own questions. In Mexico, female sterilization (tubal ligation, OTB) is the most common form of contraception by far, used by about half of women who use any method; vasectomy accounts for about 4%. Critics argue that public hospitals made sterilization after childbirth routine, with limited alternatives offered, and that Indigenous and migrant women were the most exposed. A program built on the language of free choice could still work as a program of control.

In September 2026, INEGI reported that fertility fell to 1.2 children per woman in 2024, down from 1.9 in 2019, with Mexico City at 0.8. Fifty years after “vámonos haciendo menos,” the policy question in Mexico is turning around again.

USSR: the mother as a state project

Decree of 27 June 1936

The Soviet story runs the other way. In 1920 the new Soviet state became the first in the modern world to legalize abortion on request. Early Bolshevik thinkers wanted to free women from housework and childcare by moving both into collective institutions: communal kitchens, laundries and crèches.

Under Stalin the priority became population, labor and soldiers. The decree of 27 June 1936 banned abortion except on medical grounds, with prison terms for doctors who performed illegal ones. It paid families with six or more children 2,000 rubles a year, for five years, for each additional child, and more for families with ten or more. It raised alimony, made divorce more expensive, and ordered a building program for 1936–1939 that included 400,000 new urban nursery places and 1.4 million urban kindergarten places.

In 1941 a 6% wage tax was placed on childless men aged 25–50 and married women aged 20–45. It lasted until the end of the Soviet Union. In July 1944, with the war’s losses in view, a new family law created a ladder of honors for mothers:

Mother Heroines received pension benefits, reduced utility charges and priority access to scarce goods. The honor turned childbearing into a form of public service, measured and ranked like industrial output.

The 1936 abortion ban changed the law, not the practice. Illegal abortions continued and caused an estimated 4,000 deaths a year.

Historians find that the abortion rate changed little after 1936, and that infant mortality rose between 1935 and 1940. The cash incentives did not offset it. In 1955, two years after Stalin’s death, abortion was legalized again. The medals continued. In August 2022, Vladimir Putin revived the Mother Heroine title in Russia with a payment of one million rubles.

ReadingMie Nakachi’s work on the 1944 family law traces how it was drafted and who it was meant to serve.

Where architecture comes in

Housing, nurseries, clinics

In both countries, the state’s idea of the family ended up built into housing. Unit sizes, shared facilities and the rules for who gets a home all assume a certain number of children.

Narkomfin Building, Moscow, 1928–1930

Moisei Ginzburg and Ignaty Milinis designed Narkomfin as a “transitional” communal house. Apartments had only minimal kitchen space; residents were meant to use a communal kitchen and dining room, a laundry, a gym, a library and a crèche. The architecture was designed to take domestic work and childcare out of the family apartment and share them. It was the built form of the 1920s idea of the family. When Stalin’s government turned to the large, stable family, this model was condemned, and the ground floor was later filled in with more apartments.

Social condenserConstructivist term for a building designed to change how its residents live together.

Khrushchyovka, from 1957

The decree of 31 July 1957 launched mass prefabricated housing across the USSR. The goal was one family per apartment, replacing shared kommunalki. Rooms were allocated on a simple formula of residents minus one, so a family of four got three rooms. Kitchens were 4.6 to 7.9 m². To get on the waiting list you had to have less than 4.5 m² of living space per person, and Khrushchev-era rules put families with more than five children near the front. Housing became a reward the state could give to the families it wanted.

Queue ruleSanitary minimum: 4.5 m² per person. Priority: war invalids, veterans’ families, families with more than five children.

Centro Urbano Presidente Alemán, Mexico City, 1947–1949

Mario Pani’s multifamiliar, built in the same years as the pronatalist 1947 law, was conceived as “a city within the city” for federal civil servants: apartment towers with a school, a daycare, a laundry, medical offices, sports courts, a pool and gardens. Like Narkomfin, it put services for children into the building, but here the purpose was to support the growing modern family, not to replace it.

INFONAVIT, 1972

Mexico’s workers’ housing fund was created in April 1972, funded by 5% of every formal worker’s salary, in the same years the government turned to family planning. The mass-produced units it financed in later decades were typically designed around a small nuclear family, the same household CONAPO’s logo showed. Building for small families both reflected and normalized the policy goal.

An architect who sizes a unit for two parents and two children is making a demographic assumption. Population policy shows that governments sometimes make that assumption on purpose.

Questions to keep working on

Next steps
  1. How much of Mexico’s fertility decline came from policy, and how much from urbanization, schooling and women’s work?
  2. Where is the line between offering people choices and steering them? Mexico’s sterilization rates and the Soviet housing queue are both cases to test it.
  3. Why do cash incentives and honors so rarely raise birth rates for long, from the USSR in 1936 to Russia today?
  4. With fertility now at 1.2, will Mexico turn pronatalist again, and would its housing policy change with it?
  5. For design: what would housing look like if it did not assume a single family size?
